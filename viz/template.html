Self-play reinforcement learning

Learning Clash Royale from zero

An agent that starts with random weights, plays itself two million times, and works its way up a ladder of opponents. Every number and every replay below comes from one 201-iteration training run.

This learns a simulator, not the real app. Supercell's API is read-only, automating the real client breaks their terms, and a phone plays about two matches a minute — a million matches would take roughly a year. The strategy it discovers is real; the weights do not transfer to a device.
The result

Did it actually learn?

Self-play Elo alone can lie — a policy can chase its own tail, beating last week's version while getting worse in absolute terms. So progress is measured against four fixed scripted bots that never change. This is the ungameable number.

How it learns

The loop that makes it improve

This is a genuine cycle, so the steps are numbered: each iteration feeds the next one's opponents. Playing only against the newest copy of yourself is unstable — policies drift and forget how to beat older styles — so the pool keeps history.

01
Collect
64 battles run in parallel. The agent takes one seat; a league opponent takes the other. Only the agent's transitions are kept.
02
Credit the damage
Potential-based shaping on tower-health difference gives per-tick credit for the trade; the win or loss lands as a terminal reward.
03
Update
PPO on a masked action head — 577 actions: hold, or one of 4 hand slots onto a 16×9 placement grid. Illegal plays are masked, not punished.
04
Freeze a rival
Every 20 iterations the current policy is frozen into the opponent pool, and gets an Elo rating of its own.
05
Pick hard opponents
Sampling weight (1−p)² puts the mass on opponents currently beating the agent, because that is where the gradient is.

Debugging

Two things that looked like other things

Both cost real time, and both impersonated a different bug. They are the reason the run above works at all.

01

A collapsed policy that hadn't collapsed

Entropy read 0.28 against a uniform 4.5, and KL sat at 0.00. Textbook collapse. But those two readings contradict each other — a policy that just collapsed would show large KL while it moved. The real cause: for most ticks no card is affordable, so holding is the only legal action. Three quarters of the batch was states with nothing to decide, contributing zero gradient and dragging the average down.

Confirming entropy at initialisation was correctly 5.855 ruled out the network in one step — and killed a plausible wrong theory about activation scale. The fix stores transitions only where a real choice exists, making it a semi-MDP: a span of k ticks carries Σγʲrⱼ and bootstraps with γᵏ.

entropy 0.28 → 4.43
legal actions per stored state ~1 → ~93
useful batch 25% → 100%
02

Advantage normalisation dividing by nothing

Early on, the only reward is small potential shaping, so a minibatch's advantages are nearly constant — std ≈ 0. Normalising by it turned pure noise into enormous pseudo-advantages. Now normalisation runs over the whole batch with a clamped divisor, which caps the amplification instead of letting 1/std explode.
